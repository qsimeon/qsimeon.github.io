I'm an Applied Scientist at Amazon FAI (formerly AGI), where I work on the quality of the expert-sourced data and evaluations that frontier language models are trained and measured on. Most days that means building agent-based pipelines and LLM judges that audit STEM expert data, calibrating those judges against human experts, and building RL environments for coding, STEM reasoning, and tool use. The common thread is finding the places where a dataset, or a model, is quietly wrong.

I came to this through neuroscience. I grew up in St. Lucia, came to MIT for a B.S. in Computation and Cognition, and stayed for graduate work in Ed Boyden's Synthetic Neurobiology Group, where I earned an M.S. in Computational Neuroscience. There I built neural-network models of the C. elegans nervous system, measured how they scale with data and parameters, and assembled an open dataset that unifies neural activity and connectivity across roughly 900 worms. I also spent a lot of time rebuilding a light-sheet microscope.

Two detours pulled me toward AI engineering. At Numenta I grew pretrained language models from 70 million to 1.2 billion parameters without throwing away what they already knew. At MIT's Office of Research Computing and Data I built the tooling that lets researchers run AI agents on the university's supercomputers. Somewhere in there I noticed I liked building the instruments more than running the experiments.

Now I mostly build: an agent that writes its own hardware drivers, a harness that lets a research agent search training schedules, and the Claude Code tooling I use every day. I write about it here, including the parts that didn't work.

Outside of work I read a lot of fiction, hike when I can, and travel to understand how other people live.
